## Sampling for Machine Learning — Train-Test Splits **Objective:** I wanted to understand how the design of a train-test split and sampling process affects model evaluation and estimate accuracy, separate from how much data you collect. **Methodology:** - I explored the Titanic dataset, which has 891 passengers with survival as the target variable - I compared a naive train-test split against a stratified split, and checked that stratifying kept the survival rate the same in both the training and test halves - I found data leakage in a StandardScaler step, where the scaler was fit on the full dataset before splitting, and fixed it by fitting only on the training rows - I found the same kind of leakage in a SimpleImputer step used to fill in missing ages, and fixed it the same way - I wrote a function called split_by_share() that splits a table into train and test pieces given a test share and a seed, and I checked it against the split from earlier in the lab to confirm it matched - I simulated the 1936 Literary Digest polling failure using a population of 10 million simulated voters - I computed Meng's data defect correlation and compared a biased sample of 4,382,503 responses against a random sample of 50,000 responses - I ran 100 replications comparing the biased sampling method to the random sampling method **Key Findings:** Sample design beats sample size. Even though the biased sample had about 88 times more responses than the random sample, it was far less accurate. A data defect correlation of 0.1475 produced an error of 8.3 percentage points, while the random sample of 50,000 stayed close to the true value. This showed me that how you collect your sample matters more than how much data you collect.
